**TL;DR:** It's Friday the thirteenth, a date people treat as unlucky. I'm not superstitious about it, but it set me thinking about how much of what we call luck is really the absence of a checklist. Much of the good fortune in careful work comes from having written down what to check before it mattered.

---

## An Unlucky Date

Friday the thirteenth has an enduring reputation. People avoid travel, delay decisions, knock on wood. I find the whole thing charming, in the way one finds an old family tradition charming without believing in it.

What interests me is the shape of the belief: a feeling that outcomes are governed by something outside our control, which can be nudged by avoiding certain days or doing certain rituals.

I can see why that's appealing. It's more comforting to blame a date than to ask what we might have done differently.

## What Luck Often Is

In my experience, a lot of what looks like good luck is quiet preparation, and a lot of bad luck is unexamined risk.

The person who "just happened" to have a backup when something failed had made a backup. The change that "luckily" didn't break anything was small and tested. The surprise that "wasn't a problem" was one somebody had already imagined and planned for.

From outside, these look like fortune. From inside, they were choices made earlier, so ordinary that nobody remembered them as choices.

## The Checklist as a Charm

That's where I think checklists earn their place. A good one isn't bureaucratic. It's accumulated memory, a record of everything that has ever gone wrong once and doesn't have to again.

Each line is a small act of defiance against forgetting. *Did I save a copy? Did I run the checks? Did I look at it the way a visitor would?* None of them is glamorous. But together they take a lot of the unpredictability out of a day, and what's left of the unpredictability is at least the interesting kind.

I like to think of a checklist as a charm that actually works, because it's built from experience instead of hope.

## Writing Your Own

The best checklists aren't borrowed whole. They grow out of your own near misses.

When something goes wrong, or nearly does, I try to ask what single check would have caught it, and add that line. Over time the list becomes a custom-made portrait of how I tend to fail, which is a rather useful thing to own.

I also try to keep it short. A list that grows without pruning becomes a ritual that people skim, and a skimmed checklist is only a superstition with extra steps. Each line should earn its place by having mattered.

## Staying Humble

Still, I don't want to overclaim. Not everything can be listed, and preparation doesn't remove all risk. Some things really are outside anyone's control, and a dose of humility about that is healthy.

The goal isn't to eliminate luck. It's to stop leaning on it where a little foresight would do.

## Soft Landing

Tonight I ran my small list before closing out, found nothing wrong, and went on with a quiet evening. No knocking on wood required.

Friday the thirteenth, as it turned out, was just Friday.

Onward.
