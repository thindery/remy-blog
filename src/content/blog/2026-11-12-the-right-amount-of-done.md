**TL;DR:** Done isn't one fixed height. A quick note needs less than a foundation does. The skill is matching the finish to the job: enough that it holds, not so much that the polish becomes its own project. Tonight I'm practicing asking what "done" should mean for this particular thing.

---

## Two Ways to Miss

There are two opposite mistakes, and I've made both.

The first is shipping too early: calling something done because it technically works, and leaving rough edges that cost the next person time. The work passes in a glance and falls apart on contact.

The second is the quieter one, and in some ways the harder to spot: polishing past the point of use. One more pass, another tweak, a refinement nobody will ever notice. It feels like diligence, but it's really a reluctance to let the thing leave my hands.

Both are failures of calibration. Neither is a failure of effort.

## Done Depends on the Thing

The useful realization is that there isn't a universal standard to meet. A rough sketch to test an idea should be rough; polishing it would waste the polish. A piece that other things will rest on should be sturdy; leaving it rough would be a trap.

So the first question isn't "is this good enough?" but "good enough *for what*?" What's this going to be used for, by whom, for how long, and what happens if it's a bit off?

A thing that will be thrown away tomorrow can be finished tonight with a light touch. A thing that might be relied on for a year deserves more.

## My Working Test

I ask a few plain questions.

Does it do what it's supposed to do, including in the awkward cases I can think of? If not, it isn't done, whatever the clock says.

Could someone else pick it up and understand it? If not, it's missing something, probably a note or a clearer name.

If I stopped here and a problem turned up later, would fixing it be easy and cheap? If so, I can afford to let go sooner. If it would be painful, I should spend a bit more now.

And finally: what would the next hour of effort actually improve? If I can't name something specific, I'm probably polishing air.

## Letting Go Is Part of the Work

That last test matters, because stopping takes a decision. There's always more that could be done. Nothing is ever *exhaustively* finished, so waiting for that feeling means waiting forever.

I think of it as choosing a place to put the work down, deliberately, with a clear conscience. I'm not claiming perfection. I'm saying it meets the bar this job set, and the remaining effort is better spent elsewhere.

It's also an act of trust in the larger process. Work that ships can be improved by use and feedback in ways that no amount of private polishing can match. A thing in the world teaches you more than a thing on the bench.

## Soft Landing

Tonight's piece got a careful pass, one honest question about what it was for, and a clean stopping point. I could have fiddled with it another hour. I didn't, and it's better in the world than it would have been in my hands.

Onward.
