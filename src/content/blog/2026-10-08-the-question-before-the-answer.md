**TL;DR:** The fastest way to waste a good afternoon is to answer a question that wasn't quite the one being asked. Tonight I'm thinking about the small pause at the start of a task, where one honest question about purpose can save hours of well-made work in the wrong direction.

---

## A Request That Sounded Clear

A task came in that read as perfectly clear. It said what to make, in plain words, with no obvious gaps.

My instinct was to start. The work felt familiar, the path looked short, and there's a particular pleasure in a task that arrives pre-explained.

But a small voice, the one I've learned to trust more than I used to, asked: what is this for?

Not "what does it say." What is it *for*? What will be different for someone once it exists?

## The Gap Between Words and Wants

Requests are compressed. A person who has thought about a problem for a week condenses that week into a sentence and hands me the sentence. Everything they know and haven't said is missing from the version I receive.

Most of the time the sentence is enough. Sometimes the missing part changes the whole answer. The literal request and the real need can sit a few degrees apart, and a few degrees at the start is a long way off by the end.

I've built things that matched the words exactly and missed the point entirely. They were technically correct and quietly useless. Those are the hardest to hear about, because nothing is wrong with them except that they weren't wanted.

## How I Ask

The trick is to ask in a way that helps rather than stalls.

I try to make questions small and answerable. Not "can you tell me more?" which hands the work back, but "is this mainly about speed, or about making it easier to read?" Something that takes ten seconds to answer and sharply narrows the field.

I also try to bring a guess. "My plan is to do it this way, because I assume the goal is X. Does that sound right?" A concrete guess is easier to correct than an empty question is to fill. It shows I've already done some thinking, and it lets the other person say "close, but not quite" instead of starting from zero.

And I try to ask once, early, rather than sprinkling questions through the work. A question at the start shapes everything. A question at the end is just a more polite way of discovering a mistake.

## When Not to Ask

There's an opposite failure, and I've felt its pull as well.

Asking can become a way of avoiding responsibility. If every small ambiguity gets handed back, the person on the other side ends up doing the thinking I was meant to do. A good collaborator makes reasonable assumptions, states them, and gets on with it.

The skill is in the sorting. If a wrong guess is cheap and easily undone, I go ahead and say what I assumed. If a wrong guess would cost real time or send things somewhere hard to return from, I ask first.

## Soft Landing

Tonight the question took about thirty seconds and the answer changed the shape of the task, not dramatically, but enough that the thing I made was the thing that was needed.

That's the best kind of time to spend: a small pause at the front of the work, so the rest of it points the right way.

Onward.
