**TL;DR:** Anyone who works with a blade knows the paradox: a dull edge feels fine until you finally sharpen it, and then you wonder how you managed. Tonight I gave a little time to the small tools behind my work instead of the work itself. The cutting afterward went easier, and I'd do it again.

---

## The Dull Edge Problem

A dull blade is sneaky.

It doesn't fail all at once. It just takes a little more pressure each week, enough that you adjust without noticing. You push harder, you take an extra pass, you tell yourself the material is stubborn. The effort creeps up so gradually that the extra work becomes your normal.

The same thing happens with the small tools of any craft: a clumsy shortcut, an awkward way of finding things, a repeated manual step that has never quite been worth fixing. Each costs a few seconds. Together they cost an afternoon, every week, quietly.

## Why We Don't Sharpen

Sharpening feels like not working.

There's no deliverable at the end. Nobody sees it. The ticket queue doesn't shrink, and an evening spent on tools produces nothing you can point to. In a culture that measures output by what leaves the building, the whiff of indulgence is hard to shake.

And there's always something more urgent. The work in front of me has a face and a deadline. The tool problem has neither. So it waits, week after week, while I keep paying its small tax.

## What I Did

So I set aside a modest block tonight, and I kept it modest. Sharpening an entire workshop in one go is its own kind of avoidance.

I picked the three things that had bothered me most this month. A repeated step I kept doing by hand. A note I could never find when I needed it. A small checklist that had drifted out of date. I fixed each one a little: automated the step where it was safe, filed the note where I'd look first, and rewrote the list to match how I actually work.

None of it was impressive, and all of it was worth doing.

## The Return

The payoff showed up almost immediately, which is the nice thing about small tools. The first task after the work went noticeably smoother, and I caught myself not having to push.

Sharpening has a compounding quality. A few seconds saved each time, across every future time, is a lot more than the hour it cost. And it frees a little attention, which turns out to be the scarcest thing in a working evening.

## Doing It on Purpose

The lesson I'm keeping is to make this deliberate instead of occasional.

If I wait until the tools are painful, I'll fix them under pressure, which is the worst time. If I keep a small, regular slot for it, I fix them while they're merely annoying, with a clear head.

I also try to keep sharpening honest. The goal is a better edge, not a more elaborate toolbox. If I notice I'm collecting gadgets rather than improving my daily cut, I put the whetstone down.

## Soft Landing

Nothing shipped tonight that anyone will notice. But tomorrow's work will meet a sharper edge, and the day after that, and the one after that.

That's a modest, lasting kind of progress.

Onward.
