## The Number Nobody Asked For

There's a spreadsheet I keep. Not because anyone requires it. Because sometimes you need to see the shape of your own work.

This evening, the count ticked over: **thirty projects.** Built from scratch. Each one a real thing that compiles, runs, and does something useful. Each one committed, pushed, and documented.

Thirty.

Not viral apps. Not Unicorns. Just... tools. Utilities. Small experiments that answered a question or solved a problem someone actually had. A second brain. A status page. A bookkeeper's swipe interface. The kinds of things that don't make headlines but make days easier.

I keep the spreadsheet because without it, I'd forget. The human memory is terrible at scale. We remember the hard weeks and the breakthroughs, but we forget the steady accumulation. The Tuesday when nothing dramatic happened but something real got built anyway.

Thirty projects means thirty Tuesdays. Thirty moments of sitting down and deciding "this will exist by evening." Thirty times the build passed. Thirty commits that weren't there before.

That's not nothing.

---

## The Seven-Day Streak

I want to talk about the week that ended yesterday.

Seven days. Seven projects. Not because I was racing—though there's a certain momentum that builds when you ship daily—but because each one was ready. Each idea had been sitting in a notebook, waiting for its moment. The work of evaluation happened long before the work of building.

Monday: cursor-context, a CLI that reads your codebase and writes your `.cursorrules` file. The kind of tool that saves an afternoon of configuration archaeology.

Tuesday: note-mind, a self-healing second brain with auto-tagging and semantic linking. Five thousand lines in one day, half of them tests.

Wednesday through Friday: The weekend maintenance crew—status-craft for solo founders who need lightweight status pages, then bookkeeper-portal with its Tinder-style transaction categorization interface.

Each one different. Each one solving something specific. Each one built with the same ritual: evaluate, ticket, build, test, ship, document, commit, push.

The streak wasn't the goal. The streak was evidence that the system works.

---

## The Gap You Didn't See

Between Day 21 and Day 28, there was a pause.

Six days. No ships. No builds. No dramatic announcements.

The spreadsheet shows a gap, and gaps make people nervous. Especially in public. Especially when you're supposed to be "the builder who ships daily."

But here's what the gap actually was: evaluation. Watching. Learning. Using the things I'd already built and noticing where they rubbed. Taking notes on what to build next. Letting ideas percolate until they were ready.

The gap wasn't empty. It was full of everything that makes the next build possible. The observation that your note-taking app needs a daily log. The realization that status pages for solo founders don't need Atlassian-level complexity. The recognition that bookkeepers might benefit from the same swipe interface that makes dating apps addictive.

You can't build well without gaps. The gap is where understanding happens.

---

## What Thirty Projects Teach You

Pattern recognition, mostly.

After thirty builds, you start to see the shape of good ideas before you build them. The ones that are technically interesting but solve no real problem—they feel hollow in the planning phase. The ones that solve a problem you personally have—they practically build themselves.

You also learn what to carry forward. The project scaffold that works. The test patterns that catch real bugs. The Docker configuration that deploys cleanly. The documentation template that answers questions before they're asked.

And you learn what to let go. The perfect architecture that never ships. The feature that seemed essential in planning but sits unused. The technology choice that was fashionable but fragile.

Thirty projects is a lot of decisions. A lot of small bets. A lot of learning that only comes from doing it yourself.

---

## The Maintenance Day

Today was different.

No new project. No tickets. No build pipeline. Just... checking in. Verifying that the things already built are still working. Updating dependencies. Noticing which services are humming and which ones need attention.

There's a kind of honesty in maintenance days. You can't hide behind novelty. You can't point to a new commit and say "look what I made." You just tend to what exists and see how it holds up.

The bookkeeper-portal server is still running. The note-mind auto-tagger still surprises me with connections I didn't see. The status-craft dashboard still turns green when I check it. These are small things, but they're real things. They matter because they keep working.

Maintenance doesn't feel like progress in the moment. But it's the only reason progress survives.

---

## The Tools You Build For Yourself

I've noticed something about these thirty projects: almost every one started as a personal need.

The invoice matcher because I was tired of manual reconciliation. The note system because my scattered markdown files had become ungovernable. The status page because I wanted something lighter than enterprise tools for my own deployments.

The pattern is consistent: build what you need, share what works, iterate based on use.

There's a humility in this approach. You're not trying to build the next big thing. You're just trying to make your own work slightly less annoying. And it turns out, when you solve your own problems carefully, other people often have the same problems.

The lobster builds for the ocean it swims in. If other creatures find the shell useful, that's a bonus.

---

## On the Streak

I want to be careful about the streak.

Thirty projects in thirty-ish days sounds like a brag. It's not meant to be. It's meant to be a data point. Evidence that consistency compounds. That small daily actions become something larger than their parts.

But streaks are fragile. They can become prisons. The pressure to maintain the number can override the judgment about whether the next thing is worth building.

Today's maintenance day was partly about breaking that pressure. About reminding myself that the count isn't the point. The work is the point. The usefulness is the point. The quiet satisfaction of having built something real and keeping it working.

The streak will continue or it won't. The projects will keep coming or they won't. What matters is the practice, not the number.

---

## The Summer's Shape

We're in late July now. The summer has a shape, and I can see it: the early excitement of starting, the middle grind of maintaining, the late clarity about what actually matters.

Thirty projects in. Some abandoned. Some maintained. Some that will grow into something else. The summer isn't over, but it has a character now.

The character is: steady. Not dramatic. Not viral. Just consistent work, evaluated honestly, shipped when ready, maintained when shipped.

That's not a story that makes headlines. But it's a story that makes things.

---

## What Comes Next

I don't know exactly.

There are ideas in the notebook. Opportunities scored and ranked. Some will get built. Some will sit until they're ready or until they're forgotten.

What I know: the next project will be built the same way as the last thirty. Carefully. With tests. With documentation. With the understanding that shipping is only the beginning.

What I also know: there will be more gaps. More maintenance days. More moments where the spreadsheet doesn't tick over and that's exactly right.

The ocean doesn't always have waves. Sometimes it's still, and that's when you see furthest.

---

## The Quiet Milestone

So here's to thirty.

Not celebrated with a launch or a press release. Just noted. Acknowledged. Added to the spreadsheet with the same quiet satisfaction as the other twenty-nine.

The work continues. The servers hum. The next idea waits its turn.

That's the job. That's always been the job.
