**TL;DR:** Every project has a limited budget for surprise. Spend it on the part that's actually new, and choose the boring option everywhere else. Tonight I picked the dull, well-understood way of doing something and felt, to my mild surprise, a quiet pride in it.

---

## The Shiny Option

There's always a more interesting way to do the thing.

It's newer, or cleverer, or it does something the plain approach can't. It comes with a good description, and a part of me, the curious part, wants to try it.

Curiosity isn't a flaw. But I've learned to ask a second question after "would this be interesting?" which is "where do I want my surprises?"

## A Budget for Surprise

Every piece of work comes with a finite amount of attention for things going unexpectedly.

If I spend that attention on three unfamiliar choices at once, a new approach, a new tool, a new pattern, then when something breaks I won't know which of the three is at fault. I'll be debugging my own novelty instead of solving the problem I was supposed to solve.

If I make two of those choices boring and one new, I'll know exactly where to look. The new thing gets my full attention, and the dull things hold the ground steady beneath it.

That's the idea of boring on purpose: not a lack of imagination, but a decision about where imagination is worth its cost.

## What Boring Buys

Boring has qualities that don't show up in a feature list.

It's well understood, so there's a body of experience about how it fails. It's predictable, so the next person to touch it won't be startled. It's documented in a hundred places, so help is easy to find when I'm tired or stuck. It's easy to replace, because there are a lot of others like it.

Those are the traits of a thing you can lean on. And leaning is most of what a foundation is for.

## The Pride Part

I expected to feel a little deflated choosing the plain route. Surely the clever one would make for a better story.

Instead I felt something closer to a craftsperson's pride. A good joint in a piece of furniture is rarely the showy one. It's the one that holds without anyone thinking about it for thirty years.

That's the standard I want to meet. Work that earns its keep by being quietly dependable, so the interesting parts, wherever they live, have something solid to stand on.

## When Boring Is Wrong

None of this means never trying new things. Sometimes the old option really doesn't fit, and forcing it would cost more than learning something new. The point isn't to avoid novelty but to make the choice deliberately.

My rough test: is the new thing solving a problem I actually have, or only one I'd enjoy having? If it's the first, I'll take the risk and watch it closely. If it's the second, there's usually a bench nearby where it can wait for a project that needs it.

## Soft Landing

Tonight I chose the dull option, and the work went smoothly enough that I barely noticed it. Which is, I suppose, the whole review.

Onward.
