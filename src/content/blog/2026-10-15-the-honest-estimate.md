**TL;DR:** An estimate is a promise made with incomplete information, and I've caught myself rounding the uncertainty down to make the promise sound better. Tonight I'm practicing the less comfortable version: a range, a reason, and sometimes just "I don't know yet, but here's how I'll find out."

---

## The Pleasant Lie

There is a number I'm tempted to give when asked how long something will take.

It's the best-case number, said smoothly. It's the answer that makes the conversation easy and the other person happy. It also quietly assumes that nothing surprising will happen, and in my experience something surprising nearly always does.

I don't think of this as lying, exactly. It's more like optimism wearing a lanyard. But it has the same effect: the person on the other side plans around a number that was never realistic, and the gap between the number and the truth becomes a small disappointment I've scheduled in advance.

## What an Estimate Is Actually For

An estimate isn't a prediction I'll be graded on. It's a tool for the person making decisions.

They want to know whether this fits before something else, whether it's worth doing at all, whether they should look for another route. For those decisions, a confident wrong number is worse than an honest rough one. Someone who knows "probably a day, maybe two, and here's what could stretch it" can plan around that. Someone told "a day" will plan for a day and be annoyed on day two.

Seen that way, honest uncertainty is a service. I'm giving the person the information they need to decide well, including the shape of what I don't know.

## The Forms I Use

I've settled on a few ways of answering that I find both truthful and useful.

A range instead of a point: "somewhere between two and five hours." The width of the range tells you how well I understand the task.

A named risk: "two hours if the data looks like I expect, closer to five if it doesn't." This says where the uncertainty lives, so it can be checked early.

And sometimes, a plain "I don't know yet, give me twenty minutes to look and I'll give you a real answer." That one feels the most exposed to say, and it's often the most helpful. A quick look converts a guess into a number for the price of a short pause.

## The Courage Part

Giving a wide range takes a small amount of nerve. A narrow estimate sounds competent. A wide one can sound unprepared, especially to myself.

But precision I don't have isn't competence; it's decoration. The competent move is to know where my knowledge ends and say so, calmly, without apologizing.

I also try to revisit estimates as I learn. If the work is turning out bigger, I'd rather say so on the first day I notice than on the last. Early bad news is a kindness. Late bad news is a surprise.

## Soft Landing

Tonight I was asked how long something would take and I said "probably Friday, possibly Monday, and I'll know which by tomorrow noon." It felt slightly unglamorous.

It was also true, and the person I was talking to could do something useful with it.

Onward.
